## When To Use

Use this pack when scenario changes affect:

* study objective and design alignment
* phase and development intent
* patient relevance
* critical-to-quality factors
* endpoint relevance
* population selection
* operational feasibility
* recruitment, retention, and follow-up burden
* whether the trial can answer the question it claims to answer

This pack is especially relevant for CTPredict design-coherence narratives because it connects scientific quality, patient relevance, operational realism, and study design choices.

## Key Principles

### 1. Quality should be designed into the study

Study quality should not depend only on monitoring, review, or correction after the trial starts.

A stronger design identifies the factors that matter most before execution begins and builds the protocol, procedures, data collection, and operational plan around those factors.

For CTPredict, this means the narrative should ask whether the edited trial design makes the evidence question clearer, more feasible, and more interpretable.

### 2. Quality means fitness for purpose

A trial is higher quality when it is fit for its intended purpose.

The same design choice may be strong or weak depending on whether the trial is exploratory, confirmatory, dose-finding, safety-focused, biomarker-driven, or post-approval.

The narrative should not judge design choices in isolation. It should judge whether they fit the phase, objective, population, endpoint, comparator, duration, and expected decision use.

### 3. Critical-to-quality factors should be prioritised

Critical-to-quality factors are the study attributes whose integrity is fundamental to participant protection, reliable results, interpretability, and decision-making.

The narrative should focus on the few factors that matter most, not minor procedural details.

Examples of critical-to-quality factors may include:

* clarity of the primary objective
* appropriateness of the selected population
* endpoint relevance and measurability
* bias control
* sample size and duration relative to the question
* data completeness and quality
* participant safety
* recruitment and retention feasibility
* site and investigator capability
* operational burden

### 4. Patient relevance strengthens the evidence story

A trial is stronger when the endpoint, population, visit schedule, duration, comparator, and burden are meaningful for the patients being studied.

Patient input can help clarify whether endpoints are meaningful, whether the trial duration is acceptable, whether procedures are too burdensome, and whether the trial population reflects the intended use.

For CTPredict, patient relevance should be treated as part of design coherence, not as a separate “soft” factor.

### 5. Feasibility is part of trial quality

A scientifically attractive design may still be weak if it is not operationally viable.

Feasibility includes:

* availability of the target population
* ability to recruit enough participants
* ability to retain participants through follow-up
* site capability
* regional differences in practice
* required equipment or specialist assessments
* burden on participants and investigators
* complexity of procedures and data collection

The narrative should treat operational feasibility as a design signal.

### 6. Avoid unnecessary complexity

Unnecessary procedures, excessive data collection, unrealistic visit schedules, or too many secondary objectives can reduce quality.

Complexity is not always bad. It can be justified when it protects participants, supports the primary objective, strengthens interpretation, or enables an important decision.

The narrative should distinguish justified complexity from avoidable burden.

### 7. Exploratory and confirmatory trials serve different purposes

Exploratory trials may focus on safety, dose, early efficacy signal, pharmacology, biomarker direction, endpoint selection, or defining the target population.

Confirmatory trials are expected to provide stronger evidence that the drug is safe and effective for the intended indication and population.

The narrative should avoid imposing confirmatory standards on exploratory trials, while still noting when a design claims more than it can realistically support.

### 8. The design should support meaningful comparison

A trial is stronger when the comparator, population, endpoint, and duration support a meaningful interpretation of treatment effect.

Weakness may arise when the comparator is unclear, the population is poorly aligned with the intended use, the endpoint is not meaningful, or the follow-up is too short for the outcome being assessed.

### 9. Quality factors are connected

Critical-to-quality factors should be considered together.

For example:

* broad eligibility may improve relevance but increase heterogeneity
* narrow eligibility may improve signal detection but weaken generalisability
* longer follow-up may improve endpoint maturity but increase attrition
* more sites may improve recruitment but increase operational oversight burden
* fewer sites may improve consistency but slow enrollment
* additional endpoints may enrich interpretation but increase burden and multiplicity

The narrative should explain these dependencies and trade-offs.

## Relevance To Simulator Pillars

### Phase & Intent Alignment

Use E8(R1) to assess whether the design fits the development stage.

A Phase II-style design may be coherent if it supports learning, dose selection, signal detection, or endpoint refinement. A Phase III-style design should usually show stronger alignment between endpoint, comparator, sample size, population, duration, and analysis.

### Endpoint & Evidence Strength

Use E8(R1) to assess whether endpoints are well-defined, measurable, clinically meaningful, relevant to patients, and suitable for the trial objective.

Endpoint ambition should be interpreted relative to phase and feasibility.

### Target Population Alignment

Use E8(R1) to assess whether the population reflects the research question and intended use.

Eligibility criteria should not be judged as simply broad or narrow. The key question is whether the population improves interpretability and decision value.

### Operational Burden Balance

Use E8(R1) to assess whether enrollment, site count, visit schedule, duration, data collection, and procedures are proportionate to the trial objective.

Operational burden may be acceptable when it protects safety or strengthens the primary evidence question. It is weaker when it adds complexity without improving interpretability.

### Design Coherence

Use E8(R1) to assess whether the trial forms a coherent evidence plan.

The strongest designs align objective, phase, population, endpoint, comparator, duration, operational assumptions, data collection, and expected decision use.

## Do Not Infer

Do not infer that:

* quality by design means maximal complexity
* every possible risk must be controlled equally
* every endpoint must be patient-reported
* every trial must be confirmatory
* every trial needs a large sample size
* every trial needs broad eligibility
* every operational burden is negative
* guidance can override the scenario packet
* the trial is compliant or non-compliant
* regulators would accept or reject the design

## Prompt-Safe Summary

ICH E8(R1) supports design-coherence reasoning by treating trial quality as fitness for purpose. A stronger clinical study is prospectively designed around the factors that matter most for participant protection, reliable results, interpretability, and decision-making. The trial objective, phase, population, endpoint, comparator, duration, data collection, and operational assumptions should form a coherent evidence story. Patient relevance and operational feasibility are part of scientific quality: a trial that is too burdensome, difficult to recruit, poorly aligned with patient needs, or unable to collect meaningful endpoint data may fail to answer its intended question. Critical-to-quality factors should be prioritised rather than cluttered with minor issues. Exploratory and confirmatory trials should be judged differently: early trials may focus on learning, signal detection, dose, feasibility, or endpoint refinement, while later trials usually require stronger alignment and interpretability. Use this pack to generate conditional, practical feedback on whether the design appears fit for its intended purpose.
