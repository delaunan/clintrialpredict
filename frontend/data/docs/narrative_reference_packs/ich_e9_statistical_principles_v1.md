## Key Principles

### 1. Statistical thinking starts at design, not after data collection

The statistical credibility of a trial depends on design choices made before the trial begins.

A stronger design prospectively connects:

* objective
* primary endpoint
* population
* comparator
* sample size
* bias-control methods
* analysis approach
* interpretation of results

For CTPredict, this means the narrative should identify whether the design is statistically coherent before discussing whether the trial seems likely to succeed.

### 2. Exploratory and confirmatory trials should be interpreted differently

Exploratory trials can generate hypotheses, detect signals, refine endpoints, explore doses, or inform future design.

Confirmatory trials are expected to test pre-specified hypotheses and provide stronger evidence for efficacy, safety, or another defined claim.

A design is weaker when it uses exploratory trial features but implies confirmatory strength, or when it uses confirmatory complexity without enough clarity about the main question.

### 3. The primary endpoint should carry the main evidence burden

The primary endpoint should be the endpoint most directly connected to the primary objective.

It should be clearly defined, clinically relevant, measurable, and interpretable.

Weakness may arise when:

* the primary endpoint is vague
* the endpoint is poorly aligned with the objective
* the endpoint is not clinically meaningful
* the endpoint timing is unclear
* multiple primary endpoints create ambiguity
* the endpoint is redefined after results are known
* the selected endpoint does not support the intended claim

### 4. Secondary endpoints should support, not overload, the trial

Secondary endpoints can explain, support, or enrich interpretation.

They become problematic when they create too many competing objectives, increase multiplicity, increase burden, or distract from the primary question.

The narrative should consider whether secondary endpoints add useful context or create interpretive noise.

### 5. Bias control is central to evidence strength

Bias can arise from design, conduct, analysis, and interpretation.

Important bias-control methods include:

* randomisation
* blinding
* appropriate comparator choice
* pre-specified endpoints
* pre-specified analysis
* objective endpoint assessment
* consistent follow-up
* careful handling of withdrawals
* careful handling of protocol deviations and missing data

A design is stronger when the main sources of bias are anticipated and reduced.

### 6. Randomisation supports comparability

Randomisation helps reduce bias in treatment assignment and supports comparability between groups.

For CTPredict, randomisation is relevant when interpreting the strength of causal comparison.

If randomisation is absent, limited, or not meaningful for the trial type, the narrative should avoid overstating causal inference and should consider what design features compensate for this limitation.

### 7. Blinding reduces conscious and unconscious bias

Blinding limits the influence of treatment knowledge on participant behavior, investigator decisions, endpoint assessment, withdrawal handling, and interpretation.

Open-label or single-blind designs are not automatically weak. They may be necessary or justified.

However, when blinding is limited, the design is stronger if it includes other bias-reduction measures, such as objective endpoints, central assessment, independent adjudication, pre-specified decision rules, or restricted access to treatment information.

### 8. Sample size should match the question

Sample size should be large enough to provide a reliable answer to the question being asked.

For exploratory trials, smaller samples may be acceptable if the objective is signal detection, feasibility, dose exploration, or early safety.

For confirmatory trials, sample size logic should be more explicit and should connect to the primary endpoint, expected effect, variability, event rate, type I error, type II error, withdrawals, protocol deviations, and clinical relevance.

A larger sample is not automatically better. The issue is whether the sample size is proportionate to the objective and endpoint.

### 9. Analysis should be pre-specified

Pre-specification protects interpretability.

A stronger design identifies the principal analysis approach before results are known.

Exploratory analyses are useful, but they should be interpreted as exploratory.

The narrative should distinguish between evidence that is planned and confirmatory versus evidence that is post hoc or hypothesis-generating.

### 10. Analysis sets affect interpretation

The population included in the analysis affects what question the trial answers.

Different analysis sets may support different interpretations, especially when there are protocol deviations, withdrawals, missing data, or treatment discontinuation.

The narrative should avoid assuming that one analysis set automatically gives the “true” answer. It should ask whether the analysis set is aligned with the trial objective and endpoint strategy.

### 11. Missing data and protocol deviations can weaken confidence

Missing data, withdrawals, and protocol deviations do not automatically invalidate a trial.

They become important when they affect the primary endpoint, create imbalance between arms, reduce power, introduce bias, or make the treatment effect difficult to interpret.

A stronger design anticipates these issues and explains how they will be minimised and handled.

### 12. Interim analyses and early stopping need caution

Interim analyses can support ethical or efficient decision-making, especially for safety or futility.

They can also create bias or weaken interpretability if they are not planned and controlled.

The narrative should consider whether interim decisions, adaptations, or early stopping rules are consistent with the trial objective and preserve the credibility of the evidence.

### 13. Subgroup findings are usually exploratory unless planned

Subgroup analyses can help explain heterogeneity and generate hypotheses.

They are usually less reliable when they are unplanned, underpowered, numerous, or selected after results are known.

The narrative should treat subgroup claims cautiously unless the scenario clearly supports planned subgroup reasoning.

### 14. Safety evaluation has different evidence logic

Safety and tolerability are important in all phases.

Early trials may detect only clear safety signals because sample sizes are small. Later trials can better characterise safety but may still be limited for rare events.

The narrative should avoid implying that absence of observed safety events means absence of risk.

### 15. Robustness increases confidence

A trial result is more credible when the main conclusion is not highly sensitive to reasonable changes in assumptions, analysis choices, missing-data handling, or protocol deviations.

For CTPredict, robustness should be discussed as confidence in interpretation, not as certainty of success.

## Relevance To Simulator Pillars

### Phase & Intent Alignment

Use ICH E9 to assess whether the statistical ambition fits the development stage.

Exploratory trials may tolerate flexibility and uncertainty. Confirmatory trials should have clearer hypotheses, endpoints, sample size logic, analysis plans, and bias controls.

### Endpoint & Evidence Strength

Use ICH E9 to assess whether the endpoint is primary or secondary, clinically meaningful, clearly defined, measurable, and statistically interpretable.

Endpoint strength depends on both clinical relevance and statistical credibility.

### Target Population Alignment

Use ICH E9 to assess whether the selected population supports the intended inference.

Early trials may use narrower populations to detect signal. Confirmatory trials usually need stronger alignment with the intended target population.

### Operational Burden Balance

Use ICH E9 to assess whether sample size, visit schedule, follow-up, endpoint assessments, interim analyses, and data collection are proportionate to the statistical question.

Operational burden is acceptable when it improves interpretability, bias control, safety assessment, or endpoint reliability.

### Design Coherence

Use ICH E9 to assess whether the trial’s statistical logic supports the evidence story.

A coherent design aligns objective, hypothesis, endpoint, population, comparator, sample size, bias-control methods, analysis set, missing-data strategy, and intended interpretation.

## Do Not Infer

Do not infer that:

* statistical significance proves clinical importance
* lack of significance proves no effect
* a small exploratory trial is invalid because it is underpowered for confirmatory proof
* a large trial is automatically strong
* randomisation alone removes all bias
* blinding is always possible
* open-label design is automatically weak
* every subgroup result is meaningful
* every missing value invalidates the study
* every protocol deviation invalidates the study
* exploratory analyses can support confirmatory claims
* the simulator can determine regulatory acceptability

Do not provide regulatory advice, legal advice, medical advice, or formal statistical validation.

## Prompt-Safe Summary

ICH E9 supports CTPredict narratives when design changes affect statistical credibility, endpoint strategy, bias control, sample size, analysis, missing data, or interpretation. Statistical thinking should begin at trial design: the objective, population, primary endpoint, comparator, sample size, bias-control methods, analysis approach, and intended interpretation should align. Exploratory and confirmatory trials should be judged differently. Exploratory trials may generate hypotheses, detect signals, refine endpoints, or inform future design, while confirmatory trials require clearer pre-specified hypotheses, endpoint definitions, analysis plans, and evidence standards. Bias control is central: randomisation, blinding, objective endpoint assessment, pre-specified analyses, and careful handling of withdrawals, protocol deviations, and missing data all affect confidence in results. Sample size should be proportionate to the question being asked, not simply large. Use this pack to generate practical, conditional feedback on whether the statistical design supports the intended evidence story.
